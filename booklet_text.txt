 Computer languages
 Unfortunately for us, computers can't understand spoken English or any other natural language. The only
 language they can understand directly is machine code, which consists of 1s and Os (binary code).

Machine code is too difficult to write. For this reason, we use symbolic languages to communicate
instructions to the computer. For example, assembly languages use abbreviations such as ADD, SUB,
MPY to represent instructions. The program is then translated into machine code by a piece of software
called an assembler. Machine code and assembly languages are called low-level languages because
they are closer to the hardware. They are quite complex and restricted to particular machines. To make
the programs easier to write, and to overcome the problem of intercommunication between different
types of computer, software developers designed high-level languages, which are closer to the English
language. Here are some examples:
FORTRAN was developed by IBM in 1954 and is still used for scientific and engineering applications.
COBOL (Common Business Oriented Language) was developed in 1959 and is mainly used for business
applications.
BASIC was developed in the 1960s and was widely used in microcomputer programming because it was
easy to learn. Visual BASIC is a modern version of the old BASIC language, used to build graphical
elements such as buttons and windows in Windows programs.
PASCAL was created in 1971. It is used in universities to teach the fundamentals of programming.
C was developed in the 1980s at AT&T. It is used to write system software, graphics and commercial
applications. C++ is a version of C which incorporates object-oriented programming: the programmer
concentrates on particular things (a piece of text, a graphic or a table, etc.) and gives each object functions
which can be altered without changing the entire program. For example, to add a new graphics format,
the programmer needs to rework just the graphics object. This makes programs easier to modify.
Java was designed by Sun in 1995 to run on the Web. Java applets provide animation and interactive
features on web pages.
Programs written in high-level languages must be translated into machine code by a compiler or an
interpreter. A compiler translates the source code into object code - that is, it converts the entire
program into machine code in one go. On the other hand, an interpreter translates the source code line by
line as the program is running.




It is important not to confuse programming languages with markup languages, used to create web
documents. Markup languages use instructions, known as markup tags, to format and link text files.

 The -ing form

 We use the -ing form in three ways:
     1) Rendering includes lighting and shading.

 Defining and non-defining relative clauses

    ●   We use defining relative clauses to give essential information about someone or something. It
        usually comes immediately after the noun it describes. We don’t use commas to mark its beginning
        or end.
 She is the woman who I met with yesterday.
    ●   We often leave out the relative pronoun when it is the object of the verb:
 They’re the people (that/who) she met at Jon’s office.
 Here are some bugs (that/which) the developer has identified.
    ●   We use non-defining relative clauses to give extra information about the person or thing. It is
        not necessary information. We don’t need it to understand who or what is being referred to. We
        use commas to mark the beginning and end of the non-defining relative clause. We can’t use the
        relative pronoun ‘that’ to introduce a non-defining relative clause.
 Clare, who I work with, is in charge of the project.

A little bit of History*
The Facultad Regional de Avellaneda was created on March 31, 1955 by Resolution 382/55 of the National
Commission for Learning and Professional Orientation, under the jurisdiction of the Universidad Obrera
Nacional. The establishment of this college was part of one of the objectives of the Second Five-Year Plan
of Juan Domingo Perón’s government, which aimed at the creation of institutes like this one to provide
graduates with advanced knowledge that would qualify them to lead and manage activities within the
national economic framework.
However, to fully understand its origins, we must go back a few years. On August 19, 1948, the National
Workers’ University had been created through the enactment of Law 13,329. This law designated it as an
institution of higher education “...designed to provide the solution the country was seeking: new
professionals in the field of engineering.” This was a particularly significant period in Argentina’s history,
marked by a flourishing and expanding industry, where the import substitution model was at its peak.
"The government's policy was clear: on the one hand, to create living conditions far superior to those of
the time, producing a true social revolution, and on the other, to provide specialized labor that the
emerging national industry required. A class of workers capable of deepening the country’s development,
destined to meet an increasingly important demand."
This new social context “…led a group of professionals, teachers from the Otto Krause Industrial School, to
unite in search of a high-level solution.” Thus, the objective was to:
   ●   Provide a higher education path for graduates of industrial schools or the final cycle of institutions
       under the National Commission for Learning and Professional Orientation, particularly in
       engineering and its various specializations.
   ●   Offer access to university education to a large number of students, mostly from lower-income and
       middle-class backgrounds.
The creation of the Universidad Obrera Nacional brought these goals to fruition.
However, with the rise of the 1955 dictatorship, this university—born within a clear national political
project—came under heavy scrutiny and was on the verge of disappearing. This abrupt shift in national
policy led to an institutional crisis within the Universidad Obrera Nacional and, consequently, the newly
established Facultad Regional de Avellaneda.
To address this crisis, Law 14,855, enacted on October 14, 1959, granted the university a new autonomous
legal framework, aligning it with other national universities. As a result, it ceased to depend on the former
National Commission for Learning and Professional Orientation and was renamed the National
Technological University (Universidad Tecnológica Nacional, UTN).

Unit N° 7 - Technical Report

Technical Report

A technical report is a document that clearly and in some detail, without being too lengthy, presents
the development or result of an investigation or project, enabling a quick understanding of a
complex situation.

A good report in English follows a logical structure that facilitates reading and comprehension.
Regardless of the type of report you are writing, these are the essential parts you should include:

    1. Title, Date, Problem, and Executive Summary
       It should be clear and reflect its content. Additionally, many reports begin with an executive
       summary, which is a brief overview that explicitly states the problem to be solved based on
       the options provided.
       Example:
       The Impact of Remote Work on Productivity
       The executive summary summarizes the most important points of the report in one or two
       paragraphs, including the main findings and recommendations.
    2. Introduction
       Establishes the purpose and context. This is where you explain the "why" behind your report,
       what problem you are addressing, and what you hope to achieve.
       Example:
       The aim of this report is to analyze the effects of remote work on employee productivity
       during the pandemic.
    3. Development: Body of the Report
       Presents detailed information. It is important to structure this section into different
       subsections with clear titles that reflect the content. Use headings to divide your text into
       thematic sections, making the report easier to follow.




                                                     42
                                                                                       Inglés II
                      Fernández, Ayelén - Meneghetti, Romina - Ruiz, Roberto - Estrany,
                                                         Mónica - Spizzamiglio, Carla
                                                                          Booklet Inglés II
   For example, if you are writing a report on the effects of remote work, you could divide the
   body of the report into sections such as:
       ○ Impact on employee productivity
       ○ Challenges faced
       ○ Recommendations for improvement
4. Be sure to use connectors like "furthermore," "however," and "in addition" to ensure that
   ideas flow coherently between paragraphs. The language used in reports should be formal,
   clear, and direct. This is not the time to be poetic or use colloquial phrases.
   Some key terms and expressions that will be useful when writing a report in English are:
       ○ Expressions to introduce objectives or purposes
               ■ The aim of this report is…
               ■ The purpose of this report is…
       ○ These phrases are ideal for opening your introduction and giving a clear idea of why
           you are writing the report.
       ○ Vocabulary to present information
               ■ According to the data…
               ■ The findings indicate that…
               ■ The research suggests…
       ○ These expressions are very useful for discussing facts or figures in a professional and
           objective manner.
       ○ Phrases to make recommendations
               ■ It is recommended that…
               ■ In order to improve…
               ■ It is advised to…
5. Recommendations usually appear in the final section of a report, and these phrases will allow
   you to propose actions or solutions clearly.
6. Conclusion
   The conclusion should not introduce new information but rather consolidate what you have
   already presented in the body of the report.
   Example:
   In conclusion, the analysis suggests that remote work has both positive and negative effects
   on employee productivity. It is recommended that companies implement strategies to
   improve communication and provide adequate support for remote workers.
   To be avoided:
       ○ Inappropriate use of verb tenses: Ensure that verb tenses are consistent throughout
           the report. If you are talking about past events, use the past simple, but if you are
           making recommendations or projections, the future will be your best option.
       ○ Colloquial or informal phrases: A report is a formal document, so you should avoid
           informal phrases like "gonna" or "wanna." Maintain a professional tone at all times.
       ○ Lack of connectors: Connectors are essential for your report to flow. Use words like
           "therefore," "in addition," "on the other hand" to guide the reader through your ideas.
